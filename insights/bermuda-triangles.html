Bermuda Triangles

More capital will pool in the insurance companies. It’s inevitable.

Nicholas Quaranta, Founder · November 14, 2026

A pension used to be your boss’s problem. You retire, the company pays you every month until you die. That’s over. Companies now hand the whole thing to a life insurer. They write one big check, and the insurer mails the checks from then on. Companies did about $49 billion of these handoffs last year (LIMRA).

Some people in Washington are fired up about this. Here’s the truth: it’s a better home for your retirement than most people think. An airline isn’t built to pay you for thirty years. Neither is a manufacturer. A life insurer is. That’s the whole business, and it has to hold reserves and capital against that exact promise.

How we got here

Two roads. The first is the one I just described, called pension risk transfer.

The second is the 401(k). The SECURE Acts of 2019 and 2022 made it easier to put annuities inside plans. At the end of 2025 the Labor Department said a product with an annuity in it can be a plan’s default investment. In March 2026 it proposed a rule spelling out what a company owes its workers when it picks one (Center for Retirement Research).

Both roads end in the same place: the general account of a life insurer. That’s what everyone in DC was arguing about this week. Is it right, is it fair, and where does it go from here?

It goes to Bermuda.

Bermuda

Insurers take blocks of annuity promises and reinsure them with a company offshore. Most of that traffic lands in Bermuda.

Bermuda’s long-term reinsurers held $1.52 trillion as of September 2025, and more than 80% of that business comes from the US (Insurance Business). US life insurers had more than $1.1 trillion of reserves offshore by the end of 2024. Bermuda took over 60% of new deals in 2023 and 2024.

About 70% of that went to reinsurers owned by the same group as the insurer that sent it (Insurance Business, citing AM Best and ALIRT). Regulators don’t love that because the risk isn’t leaving the family and more families are coming to play the game.

In 2011 there were 16 privately owned life insurers. By the end of 2025 there were 93. That number is going up over the next decade, and at CMF we want one. Their share of the industry’s invested assets went from 2.5% to 19.8%, about $1.2 trillion. Apollo, KKR, Ares, Brookfield and Carlyle are all playing in Bermuda and the reason is simple. Every block an asset manager moves offshore frees up capital and grows the pile of money it earns fees on. And freed-up capital is exactly what lets these insurers pay better annuity rates and bid lower on pension buyouts.

The fair criticism

Things get harder to see once a block crosses the border, and that’s pretty much a disclosure problem, and it’s getting fixed.

Start with the reserves. A state insurance commissioner watches the US insurer. Once the promises sit in Bermuda, a different regulator uses a different rulebook. The first US filings built to look through these deals came in the second quarter of 2026, and regulators found that many annuity blocks carry lower reserves after the move than US rules would require (InsuranceNewsNet).

That’s not a scandal, though. That’s the point of the move. US statutory reserves are conservative on purpose, and plenty of companies reported they still hold 100% of the US amount after the transfer. Regulators haven’t found anyone short.

Then the assets. A thirty-year promise is only as good as the money behind it. Onshore, a regulator can open the books and trace every investment. Offshore, US regulators are still asking for that view. After the first filings, they said they want more transparency, they’ve started verifying Bermuda balance sheets, and they’re reviewing assets with no clear market price. Bermuda has already expanded its own disclosure rules along US lines.

That last bucket is big. US life insurers held about $807 billion in private credit and other illiquid investments, up from $685 billion a year earlier. Investments tied to related companies ran from about 12% of assets at Athene to more than 40% at some others. When the reinsurer, the asset manager and the lender all share an owner, the public deserves a clear answer on which assets back which promises. Giving that answer costs these firms almost nothing and buys them a lot of trust.

Bermuda isn’t the only exit either. A US insurer can get a similar result without leaving the country by ceding business to a captive, a sister reinsurer in a state that lets it hold reserves differently. State regulators approve those one at a time.

The money follows the promises, and that changes two things about American capital markets. Both are improvements.

First, who’s putting up the capital. Growth in this business has mostly come through acquisitions and reinsurance, often with foreign entities. Fresh money backing American retirements is increasingly raised by private owners and put into Bermuda companies instead of coming from US insurers selling stock and bonds. That’s new money standing behind retirees, from owners who aren’t managing to a quarterly share price.

Second is what the premiums buy. Insurers used to own mostly public corporate bonds with a price you could look up. Five things changed that:

  • Low rates broke the old model. For over a decade, insurers had promised returns that public bonds couldn’t pay. Private loans pay extra for being harder to sell.
  • Banks walked away. After 2008, new rules (Dodd-Frank, Basel III) made it expensive for banks to hold middle-market loans, asset-backed deals and commercial real estate. Insurers with thirty-year promises were the natural buyers.
  • Asset managers bought the insurers. Apollo started building Athene in 2009, and KKR, Brookfield and Carlyle followed. Their whole skill is making private loans, so they point the premiums at loans they source themselves and get paid on both ends.
  • The capital rules reward it. US capital charges mostly follow a security’s rating, not how easy it is to sell. A privately rated loan can carry the same charge as a public bond with the same rating, and pay more.
  • Competition spread it. The PE-backed insurers used the higher yield to offer better rates and win pension deals. Everyone else had to follow or lose share.

So more of the money now sits in private loans and structured credit. US insurers held $276.8 billion of collateralized loan obligations at the end of 2024, about 5.1% of their bonds. None of it trades publicly, so its value comes from models and private ratings.

Here’s why the model works. Annuity promises are long and predictable, so an insurer can hold assets that are hard to sell and collect the extra yield. A bank funds a ten-year loan with deposits that can walk out the door tomorrow. An insurer funds it with promises that come due over decades. Moving long-term lending from banks to insurers puts it with a lender that can’t get run on. Private credit has also been in life insurers’ portfolios for decades (ACLI). And the cash is there: Bermuda’s reinsurers reported a median liquidity coverage ratio of 471% under a severe stress test in 2024, against a 105% minimum.

So what?

The case for this model is strong. The US insurer that sold the contract stays on the hook even after it reinsures the risk. Forced recaptures in life and annuity reinsurance have been rare, a point the American Academy of Actuaries made to regulators this fall. And Bermuda’s reported liquidity is way above the minimum.

But let’s take Bermuda out of the equation. Bermuda could be anything in the future. I get the sense that people like to point to it because it adds a shadowy, worrisome feel that helps regulators sell fear.

This new system works. That’s the truth. You give your money over to a bank because you feel like you need liquidity fast. For most people that’s the truth, but for others the banking system was rendered obsolete 25 years ago. More capital will pool in the insurance companies. It’s inevitable. It’s up to you to figure out which game you want to play.

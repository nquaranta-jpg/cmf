Sold, Not Bought

Finance is where AI agents will matter most. It will just get there last.

Nicholas Quaranta, Founder · November 22, 2026

In Stripe’s annual letter this year, Patrick and John Collison laid out five levels of agentic commerce. At the bottom, you tell an assistant exactly what to buy and it fills in the form. At the top, level five, the assistant already knows your needs and your budget, and it acts before you ask. Their read was that we’re somewhere between levels one and two, and that this will arrive in small chunks rather than all at once. Since then they’ve gotten a lot more bullish. At Stripe Sessions in April, Patrick said that in the not-too-distant future agents will account for most transactions online, and the first brands lining up to sell through agents were retailers like Best Buy and Coach (Stripe).

For now that makes sense, because those things are the easiest for agents to find and shop for. But when I buy clothes I want to feel them and see how they look on me. Amazon’s 3D try-on never helped me in that regard. And when I had my agent look for Airbnbs in DC, the suggestions it gave me were appalling. My suspicion is that agents top out in these categories about where they are now. My guess is that the category where agents end up mattering most is consumer finance, and that it will be one of the last to arrive.

Why finance

A financial product is a contract. Once the terms and the price are known, everything about it can be compared on paper, and reading terms and comparing numbers is what software is best at. Term life, auto insurance, savings rates, credit cards and refinancing all work this way. Nobody needs to try on a term policy.

There’s a second reason, and it may matter more. A large share of the profit in consumer finance comes from people not shopping around. Money sits in an account paying a fraction of the going rate. The auto policy renews every year without anyone checking. Call it an inattention tax.

An agent that re-shops everything, continuously and at no effort to you, removes that tax. Finance is where agents could move the most money, precisely because today’s customers are the least attentive.

What slows it down

A few things cut against this, at least for now.

The early data points elsewhere. McKinsey’s survey this October found apparel leading the purchases AI has actually completed. Worldpay’s research found people most comfortable handing agents small digital purchases, and more willing to delegate bigger amounts on travel than in other categories. People delegate first where the downside is small.

The price isn’t known up front. With a shirt, the price is on the tag. With life insurance, the real price only exists after underwriting, so “buy it if the price is right” can’t be judged at the moment of purchase. Whoever closes that gap, by telling you before you apply what you’ll actually qualify for and pay, unlocks the category.

The law requires a person. An application needs the applicant’s own health answers, consent and signature, and the sale needs a licensed party. An agent can prepare everything, but the insured still has to attest.

Mistakes are expensive and slow to surface. A wrong shirt gets returned. A wrong answer on an application can sink a claim years later, and someone has to carry that liability.

Not all of it is a commodity. Term life is close to one. Indexed universal life and whole life are not. They have moving parts, long horizons and suitability rules, and they’re where an agent’s judgment is most likely to be questioned.

Nobody goes looking for it. The old line in this business is that life insurance is sold, not bought. An agent that can complete a purchase doesn’t help if the person never asks. That changes at level five, when an assistant manages someone’s finances broadly enough to notice the gap on its own.

Where the value goes

If term life becomes something agents compare instantly, it will get priced like a commodity. Margins and commissions on the simplest products will come under pressure, the way they did for airline tickets and stock trades once comparison became free. The value moves to whoever the assistants route through.

In a market where agents shop on terms and price, two things become scarce: an accurate, trustworthy answer about who qualifies for what, and someone licensed to complete the sale. The obstacles that will slow agents down in finance are legal and procedural, not technical. And those same obstacles are going to favor the people who are already licensed over the newcomers with a slick app.

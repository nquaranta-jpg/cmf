Gunboats and GPUs

The bankers ran Cleveland’s cabinet. Silicon Valley runs this one.

Nicholas Quaranta, Founder · November 27, 2026

Over this Thanksgiving break I was reading Murray Rothbard’s Wall Street, Banks, and American Foreign Policy. Rothbard was an anarchocapitalist who believed the state was a criminal enterprise. He was an economist out of the Austrian school, a student of Ludwig von Mises, and the guy who coined the term “anarcho-capitalism.” In Anatomy of the State he called the government “a gang of thieves writ large,” and he spent his career tracing who actually profits when it acts. This book is that project applied to foreign policy: follow the bankers, and you find the wars and the markets.

The book starts with Grover Cleveland’s cabinet, and it was basically a JP Morgan org chart. I personally had no idea how much of an influence bankers had on our administrations until about the ’90s in this country. Cleveland’s Secretary of War married into the Peabody family, and a Peabody had been best man at J.P. Morgan’s wedding. The Navy Secretary was a New York financier tied to the Morgans’ railroad interests. His successor was so close to Mayer Lehman that Lehman named a son after him.

You can read this and be appalled, or take it at face value for what it is and what it did for this country. Rothbard argued that this circle of bankers turned America from a country that minded its own business into one that went abroad looking for markets. That expansion fueled growth, innovation and capitalism, and turned us into the world’s dominant superpower.

Cleveland served twice, from 1885 to 1889 and from 1893 to 1897, and both of his cabinets came out of New York finance. William C. Whitney, a financier, ran the Navy in the first term and began rebuilding the steel fleet. In the second term his protégé Daniel Lamont ran the War Department, and Hilary Herbert, the Lehmans’ friend, took over the Navy and kept the shipyards busy.

The clearest sign of the banker influence came in 1895. The Panic of 1893 had set off a deep depression, and the Treasury’s gold reserve was almost gone. A syndicate led by Morgan and August Belmont, the Rothschilds’ agent in America, rescued the government with a private gold-bond deal. The United States had to go to a bank to stay solvent.

Foreign policy hardened around the same interests. Business leaders worried that American farms and factories were producing more than Americans could buy, so they wanted markets abroad. Alfred Thayer Mahan’s 1890 book on sea power gave them a strategy built on fleets and coaling stations. By 1894 the Navy was helping break a rebel blockade of Rio de Janeiro. In 1895 Secretary of State Richard Olney told Britain that the United States was “practically sovereign” in the Western Hemisphere. Historians still argue about how directly the bankers drove the gunboats, but nobody disputes who held the gold.

I don’t think anyone would disagree that the new power broker is Silicon Valley. But unlike the bankers of the Cleveland era, it’s hard for me to sit here and really believe they have the country’s interest at heart.

Read what they write. In Machines of Loving Grace, Anthropic’s Dario Amodei imagined AI compressing a century of scientific progress into a decade, and argued the democracies have to win the race. This January, in The Adolescence of Technology, he predicted AI could wipe out half of entry-level white-collar jobs within one to five years, and asked companies to please reassign people instead of firing them. Sam Altman’s The Gentle Singularity opens with “We are past the event horizon; the takeoff has started,” and says superintelligence shouldn’t be “too concentrated with any person, company, or country.” Any country. Including this one. These are essays written for humanity and the species and the future. Whitney rebuilt the Navy for the United States. I’m not sure who these guys are building for.

Then look at who sits close to the president. In March the White House named Mark Zuckerberg, Larry Ellison, Jensen Huang and Sergey Brin to its science and technology advisory council, and Marc Andreessen and David Friedberg joined them. When Elon Musk left DOGE, the rest of the industry moved into the space he left. According to Public Citizen, tech companies and executives gave about $35 million to a record inauguration fund. These are the Whitneys and Lamonts of our time.

Foreign policy shows it most clearly. In the 1890s the argument was that America needed foreign markets for its surplus goods. Today the argument is about AI chips. Huang lobbied hard to reopen chip sales to China. When the president approved H200 exports last December, he said it would “support American Jobs” and “strengthen U.S. Manufacturing.” Nvidia said it would “support high paying jobs and manufacturing in America.” One company’s sales strategy became the country’s position toward its main rival. That is exactly Rothbard’s pattern, with a chip designer in Santa Clara in the place the banker on Wall Street used to hold.

The strongest evidence that Wall Street is fading is how it makes its money now. Its biggest growth business is financing tech. Morgan Stanley’s debt underwriting revenue rose 93% in a single quarter, driven by AI borrowing like the $27 billion it arranged for Meta’s Hyperion data center. JPMorgan, Goldman, Morgan Stanley and Citi have all built dedicated teams to win data center deals, and JPMorgan’s head of investment banking calls the AI buildout the “largest investment cycle in the history of capitalism.” In 1895 the government came to Morgan. In 2026 Morgan Stanley comes to the hyperscalers.

Skeptics will point out that the Treasury Secretary came from a hedge fund and the Commerce Secretary from Cantor Fitzgerald, so finance must still be running things. But consider what those men spend their days on. Commerce writes the chip export rules, and Treasury negotiates the trade deals that decide where Nvidia can sell. The financiers in the cabinet now spend their time administering a tech economy.

Others will say Washington holds the leash. They point to the government’s stake in Intel, the 15% revenue share on some chip sales to China and the 25% cut on others. That actually supports my point. Governments tax whatever produces the wealth, and Washington is collecting from Silicon Valley, not Wall Street.

The Jensen Huang scandal settles it. On November 12 he resigned as Nvidia’s CEO after reports of an affair with Alice Huang, a former executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore company under investigation over whether it helped Chinese firms get their hands on Nvidia chips. Within a week, Commerce closed a loophole that had let Chinese firms rent advanced U.S. chips in data centers abroad. One executive’s private life became a national security event and changed export policy in days. It’s hard to think of a banker today whose personal life would carry that much strategic weight. So much so that eleven days later Huang was back in power, after Musk, the rest of the valley and the president himself pushed the board to bring him back.

So where does this go?

If you boil Rothbard down to one rule, it’s this: power goes to whoever holds the thing the state can’t do without. In 1895 that was gold. Today it’s compute. So the real question is who holds it ten years from now, and what Washington does about it. I see five ways this plays out.

Silicon Valley becomes the new Morgan. This is where we currently are. A handful of companies become things the government can’t function without, and instead of regulating them Washington buys in, taking stakes and revenue cuts. Wall Street becomes the contractor that finances the data centers.

The debt comes due. This is the one nobody’s talking about. The whole buildout runs on borrowed money: bonds, private credit, project finance, much of it sitting with banks, asset managers and insurers. If the returns don’t show up, it’s 2008 again. Whoever holds the debt decides who survives, and the government decides who gets rescued.

The Manhattan Project. If AI ends up treated like nukes, the government takes over the labs a piece at a time: security clearances, classified models, Washington-approved boards. Silicon Valley becomes Lockheed Martin, rich and powerful but working for the Pentagon. The China rivalry pushes this way, and the Huang affair was a preview.

The labs outgrow the state. Amodei calls what he’s building a “country of geniuses in a datacenter.” If one company has more brainpower than the federal government, this stops being a story about donors and politicians and starts looking like two powers negotiating. Rothbard has nothing for this one, because no bank was ever smarter than the government.

The voters revolt. If half of entry-level white-collar jobs really disappear, people turn on Wall Street and the valley at the same time. You get windfall taxes and some kind of AI dividend paid out to citizens, and the chip revenue shares are the early version of that. Amodei himself is already arguing for a tax on AI profits, which tells you some of them see it coming.

Which one we get comes down to a few things. Do the models turn into a commodity, so the power moves to whoever owns the chips and the electricity? Does the AI debt pay off? How hot does it get with China? How fast do the jobs go?

My bet is that in most of these, the winners are the people sitting on long-term capital, the insurers and the private credit funds, either as the ones financing the buildout or the ones bailing it out.

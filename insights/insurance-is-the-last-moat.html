Insurance Is the Last Moat

In 1990 the place to build a fortune on your own terms was Wall Street. Today it’s insurance.

Nicholas Quaranta, Founder · November 8, 2026

In the ’80s and ’90s, if you wanted to get rich, you went to Wall Street. Mike Milken, Leon Black, Steve Cohen and plenty of others went there and built fortunes from nothing. They operated inside the shell of big institutions, but there was an important point in that: you could get rich on your own terms.

Now, with algorithmic trading and the explosion of private credit, there are very few places a young person can go to build a fortune on his own terms. IB and PE are dead. Private credit is too big; you could never compete with Apollo. And yes, you can work at Google or Nvidia, build a network and leave to spin out your own firm. But good luck competing with the AI labs, where it’s a race to build “the one.”

Insurance is the last moat

Look at where some of the biggest fortunes of the last 35 years actually came from, and an insurance balance sheet keeps showing up underneath them.

Leon Black and Executive Life. When Drexel collapsed in 1990, Black had no deals left to do. Executive Life, a California insurer that had been one of Milken’s biggest junk bond buyers, failed and was seized by state regulators in 1991. That November, a group backed by the French bank Credit Lyonnais, with Black as its adviser, won the auction for the bond portfolio: about $3.2 billion for bonds with $6.1 billion of face value. Many of the best bonds landed in Apollo’s early funds. When the junk market came back, Apollo was made. Today Apollo owns Athene, one of the largest annuity companies in the country.

Guggenheim and the Dodgers. Guggenheim, run by Mark Walter, bought Security Benefit, a struggling Kansas annuity company, in 2010, then added more insurers. In 2012 Walter’s group bought the Los Angeles Dodgers for a record $2.15 billion. A later class action claimed the insurers Walter and Todd Boehly controlled supplied more than half that money (Stacker); Guggenheim denied it and the complaint was withdrawn. This year, federal prosecutors and the SEC are looking at whether two Walter-controlled insurers, Delaware Life and Clear Spring Life, classified private credit tied to his other businesses as unaffiliated. Delaware Life has since said its related-party investments total more than $17 billion, not the roughly $1.4 billion it had reported, and plans to cut up to $6.5 billion of those holdings. Meanwhile Walter agreed to sell the Lakers, which he bought control of in 2025 at a $10 billion valuation, for a reported $12.5 billion, about $2.5 billion more (NBC Sports).

Buffett did a cleaner version of the same thing decades earlier, using insurance float to build Berkshire.

Look, clearly these guys aren’t saints. But if there’s a lesson here, it’s this: insurance is the one place where one person can still end up controlling an enormous, patient pool of capital. And unlike trading or private credit, the front of the business still can’t be automated or bought outright. On the life and annuity side it’s still very much a people business, and it will stay that way even if we go all the way down the AI rabbit hole. If you don’t believe me, spin up a little AI bot with a ChatGPT voice and have it cold call business owners about life insurance. You’ll waste your time and money, and maybe even get sued.

Life insurance, even more than real estate, is a product that is sold, not bought. And as private credit needs more premium to fund more deals, and as life gets more precious in the wake of automation, the premium flowing into this industry will keep growing.

So when my brokerage sells someone an annuity or a life policy, I’m not really selling them an insurance product anymore. I’m selling them a seat in one of the biggest lending machines on Wall Street. Over the last fifteen years, life insurance, private credit and asset management have fused into one business. The insurer gathers long-term money. The private credit desk lends it out. The asset manager collects fees on the whole loop. In a lot of cases, all three sit under the same parent company.

Most people buying a policy have no idea this happened. Most agents selling one don’t think about it either. I think every agent should, because it changes what you’re actually putting your clients into, and where this industry is going next.

And where is that? Let me tell you.

More crypto products: In January 2026, Delaware Life launched the first fixed index annuity with bitcoin exposure, using a BlackRock index that blends U.S. stocks with bitcoin (The Block). Yes, that’s Walter’s insurer. It won’t be the last. Expect bitcoin-linked IULs and annuities, coverage built for people who hold crypto, and policies with digital assets inside them. A generation that grew up on Coinbase is going to expect it.

Retail investors start trading insurance. Today, buying someone’s life policy on the secondary market, a life settlement, is mostly an institutional game. That’s changing. Companies are already putting life settlements on the blockchain in small, tradeable pieces, and the Department of Labor proposed a rule in March 2026 to help 401(k) plans add private assets (Morrison Foerster). Put those together and policies, annuity blocks and the private credit behind them become something a regular person can buy in an app.

You’ll be able to gamble on life products. Prediction markets now price almost everything. Kalshi has listed a contract on U.S. life expectancy, and hurricane contracts on Kalshi and Polymarket already work like a form of insurance (Insurance Journal). For now, U.S. rules bar contracts tied to war, terrorism or assassination, and Kalshi got sued this year after invoking its “death carveout” on a $54 million market. But a life settlement is already a bet on how long someone lives. The line between a policy, an investment and a wager is thinner than people think, and someone is going to build a market right on it.

Distribution gets bought. The balance sheets are consolidated. The loan origination is consolidated. The piece that’s left is the people who actually sell. Private equity has already rolled up most of the big marketing organizations. Agencies that can deliver steady, clean, compliant premium are going to be worth a lot of money to whoever needs to fill those balance sheets.

MCP agents run the pipes between agency and carrier. MCP is an open standard that lets AI agents plug directly into software. Most agents still log into a half dozen carrier portals, retype the same client data and wait on email. At CMF we’ve already moved past that. Our agency app is wired to AI agents over MCP, and those agents work between us and the carriers: pulling quotes, filling apps, chasing signatures, tracking underwriting and keeping our pipeline current without anyone babysitting a portal. The rest of the industry will get here eventually. The agencies that wire in first will move faster and cheaper than everyone else, and the agent’s job shrinks down to the part that matters: the conversation that gets someone to buy.

Put it all together and the value in this industry is moving to two places: the balance sheet at the top and the relationship at the bottom. Apollo already owns the top. The bottom is still wide open. And the stranger the products get, with crypto inside annuities and policies trading like stocks, the more people will need someone they trust to explain what they’re actually buying.

This is why I’m building Crown Merchant Financial. In 1990 the place to build a fortune on your own terms was Wall Street. Today it’s insurance.
